# "The Town That Couldn't Stop Dancing" (TOON3D style 1: sunny riverside town) — original script v1 (2026-11-03)
Facts: Strasbourg, July 1518 ("dancing plague"). Frau Troffea began dancing in the street and kept going for 4-6 days;
within a week ~34 people had joined, within a month accounts say ~400. The council, advised by physicians that it was
"hot blood", built a stage, hired musicians and paid strong men to keep dancers moving, which drew in more dancers;
later they banned public music and dancing and sent the dancers to a shrine of St Vitus near Saverne, after which it
faded. Leading explanation: mass psychogenic illness under famine, disease and fear (John Waller, "A Time to Dance,
a Time to Die", 2008; Wikipedia "Dancing plague of 1518"). Exact numbers are from chronicles and are uncertain.
Narrator: Jett (fast, steady), eleven_multilingual_v2, native speed 1.1 (~3.7 words/s). No captions.

In 1518, a woman started dancing in the street, and a whole town couldn't stop.
It's July in Strasbourg, and Frau Troffea steps out of her house and just starts dancing. No music. No smile.
Her husband begs her to stop. She can't. She dances for six days straight.
By the end of the week, thirty neighbors are dancing with her.
By August, records say it's hundreds.
So the town council meets, and they come up with a plan.
If they can't stop dancing, let them dance it out.
They build a stage, hire a band, and pay strong young men to keep the dancers moving.
It does not help. The music just pulls in more dancers.
You see, the town was starving, sick and terrified, and fear spreads through a crowd like a yawn.
Ever watched one person yawn, and then the whole room yawns too? Nobody chooses it. It just jumps.
One frightened dancer became thirty, and thirty became hundreds.
So the council finally does the opposite. They ban music in public, and send the dancers to a quiet shrine in the hills.
And slowly, the dancing stops.
The town cured a dancing plague by doing the one thing nobody at a party ever does: turning off the music.
